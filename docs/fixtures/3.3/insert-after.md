# Three Kinds of Revision

## Line edits

Line edits are the kind most people picture when they hear "revision." You tighten a sentence, swap a verb, cut an adjective, close up a clause. The prose gets denser; nothing structural moves.

Good line editing is mostly about removing words. Every sentence you shorten without losing meaning is a sentence that reads twice as fast.

## Structural edits

Structural edits are different. You are no longer sharpening sentences; you are moving sections, rewriting transitions, cutting whole paragraphs that turned out to be throat-clearing. The document's skeleton changes.

Structural edits are harder because they require holding the whole piece in your head at once. This is why most writers avoid them. It's also why most writing doesn't earn its length.

## Developmental edits

Developmental edits sit above structural ones. At this level you are questioning whether the piece should exist at all in its current shape — whether the argument holds, whether the angle is right, whether the thing you set out to write is actually the thing in front of you.

A developmental edit almost always shortens the document. Sometimes it replaces it.

## Why the order matters

The instinct is to start with line edits because they are cheap and feel productive. The mistake is that a line edit inside a paragraph you will later cut is wasted work. Always do the structural pass first, then the lines.

Your first draft wants you to cut it. Let it.
