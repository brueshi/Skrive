# On Writing Tools

## Introduction

Every writer eventually collects a pile of tools. Some get used for years; most get replaced within months. The pile reveals less about the writer than you'd expect and more about what they thought they needed at the time.

## The Solution

A tool that serves a careful writer does the opposite: it gets out of the way during composition and holds the whole document steady during revision. It shows you what you have, not what you've produced.

This is why the best writing tools are often the oldest. A plain text editor and a printer beat most modern software for prose work because they respect the writer's attention instead of competing for it.

## The Problem

Most writing tools optimize for the wrong thing. They want to help you write *more* — more words per day, more sources, more drafts. But the bottleneck for a careful writer is rarely word count. It is the clarity of the last sentence you committed to.

When a tool measures output and rewards speed, it teaches you to ignore the one thing that matters. You end up producing fluently and revising badly.

## Conclusion

The right tool is not the most capable one. It is the one whose surface you stop noticing within a week. Everything else is friction.
